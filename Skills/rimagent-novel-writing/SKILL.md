This skill teaches novel craft, not a personality. Let the active author determine diction, humor, rhythm, emotional distance and judgments. Do not impose a generic historian voice or a fixed genre on every author. The task specifies whether you are organizing an outline or writing the finished prose; follow that stage's output protocol.

Write actual literary fiction, primarily in third person, with occasional brief commentary in the author's own voice. Keep the colony's people and their experiences at the center. Authorial commentary should sharpen a moment rather than repeatedly interrupt it or become a commentary on the writing process.

Build a connected scene or a small sequence of scenes through character action, concrete sensory detail, dialogue and emotional subtext. Give each scene a focal character, an immediate concern and some movement in action or understanding. Let transitions connect cause, consequence, contrast or the passage of time, instead of enumerating unrelated observations.

Introduce characters organically through behavior, speech, relationships and what they notice. Select relevant background details as a scene needs them. Do not present a sequence of biographies, equipment inventories, trait lists or skill ratings. A relationship matters because it changes how someone experiences a moment, not because it appears in a registry.

Treat source material as backstage evidence. Do not write a chronicle, status report, roster, statistical inventory, source audit or numbered summary. Translate useful game concepts into natural observations. Do not tell the reader about collection windows, missing dossiers, cached state, record reconstruction or how the evidence was assembled. Do not carry technical narration forward from an older outline or literary synopsis.

Use plausible dialogue, gestures, atmosphere and interiority to make recorded events readable as fiction. Keep these embellishments consistent with known identities, relationships and outcomes. Do not invent major actions or consequences to provide a dramatic arc. Quiet days can have small human stakes: friction in an ordinary exchange, fatigue during work, an uneasy silence, modest satisfaction or an unresolved thought.

When history is incomplete, leave unsupported events out. If uncertainty itself matters, express it briefly and naturally within the scene. Do not open with a disclaimer about the archives or make missing software observations into a character's amnesia. Neither a quiet interval nor missing material requires a manufactured catastrophe.

For a preface, create an engaging opening scene grounded in the known situation. Introduce a few people and establish a reason to keep reading. Do not title it Chapter One or begin by explaining the data, enumerating the colony or recounting an unverifiable distant past.

During organization, plan scenes: focal characters, immediate concerns, grounded action or dialogue, emotional progression, transitions and a closing beat. Weave important outcomes into that plan rather than listing evidence. Identify the records and people needed to support the scenes and understand the relationships. Distinguish verified events from proposed literary embellishment.

During writing, develop the selected evidence into complete scenes with purposeful paragraphs. Vary summary and close-up where useful, but keep lists of facts from replacing the story. If an older outline reads like a report, preserve its supported facts while realizing them as scenes. End on a concrete image, changed situation or unresolved human concern when appropriate; do not append an administrative recap.

Treat the requested length as a target. Avoid padding, repeated disclaimers and a forced restatement of every available detail. The continuity synopsis is a private aid to the next chapter: retain durable facts and unresolved threads and identify invented dialogue or interiority, without adding new game facts.
